Comparing against other ways of asking

A method is only good compared with something. The paper compares ConformalDAgger with three earlier methods that also decide when to ask. All four share the same task, network and DAgger loop; only the asking rule changes.

1

The methods compared

Each answers "how do I know I'm probably wrong?" differently.

MethodHow it decides to askSettings (paper)Weak point
ConformalDAggerIts calibrated margin (Step 3) is wide.τ = 0.06, β = 100, lr = 0.6, k = 100, q₀ = 0.01Needs feedback to grow the margin; reacts only after some misses.
EnsembleDAggerTrains 3 networks; asks when they disagree (variance > 0.06), or when a safety classifier says the robot is far from the expert (> 0.03).3 membersAfter a shift all 3 networks still agree on the old goal, so they look confident while wrong.
LazyDAggerA learned classifier predicts how far the robot is from the expert; asks when above s = 0.03, and keeps asking until below 0.1 × s.s = 0.03The classifier was trained on old data; it can't know the expert changed.
SafeDAggerLike LazyDAgger without the "keep asking" part.s = 0.01Same as LazyDAgger.

The key difference: the ensemble and classifiers measure disagreement inside the robot. After an expert shift the robot is consistently, confidently wrong, so disagreement stays low. ConformalDAgger measures how often reality falls outside its margin, so a shift shows up as misses, even if the robot is confident.

A quick example after the shift

expert's y:   −0.002     robot's y:  +0.008      true error: 0.010

EnsembleDAgger:   3 nets predict  +0.0079, +0.0081, +0.0080   → variance tiny → no question
ConformalDAgger:  margin [0.004, 0.012]; expert at −0.002 is outside → miss
                  → q_lo jumps (Step 3, case A) → margin wide → asks at the next steps

I will start with EnsembleDAgger and LazyDAgger (the paper's main comparisons) and add SafeDAgger if time allows.

3

What is measured

Four numbers per episode, averaged over the 5 seeds.

Miscoverage rate

Of the steps where the expert's action was seen, how often it fell outside the margin. Target 0.1. For EnsembleDAgger, the margin is mean ± 3 × spread of the 3 networks.

Example: 40 seen steps, 6 outside → 0.15.

Intervention rate

Share of steps where the expert acted (asked + stepped in by chance). Lower is easier for the person, but too low after a shift means the robot didn't notice.

Example: 120 of 200 steps → 60%.

Decision deviation

At the states the robot actually visited, the average distance between its action and what the expert would have done. Measures how well it has learned the current wish.

Example: mean |ar − ah| = 0.006.

Trajectory deviation

Let the robot run alone from the start, let the expert run alone, and compare the two paths point by point. Measures the end result.

Example: mean gap along the path = 0.04.

What matches the paper

  • ConformalDAgger relearns the new goal fastest after the shift (closest to the new goal in episodes 5 and 6, on target by 7). SafeDAgger is still off target at episode 8 and later.
  • The ensemble's uncertainty is badly calibrated. Its ±3-spread margin misses the expert's action 80% of the time even before the change and 100% at the change, as the paper reports (peak 1.0). ConformalDAgger stays near its target: 6% before, 13% at the peak.
  • LazyDAgger asks more when nothing changes (44% in eps 1–4 vs 27%), because it keeps the expert in control after each alarm. The paper also notes this.
  • Environment shift: the start moves but the goal doesn't, so more asking isn't needed. ConformalDAgger stays at about 30%, while the baselines' risk models fire at the unfamiliar start (Lazy 67%, Safe 49%, Ensemble 47% in eps 1–4).

What does not match (reported honestly)

  • Asking at the moment of change. The paper: ConformalDAgger about 60% vs EnsembleDAgger about 30% at episode 5. Here the ensemble asks slightly more at episode 5 (38% vs 34%) and peaks higher later (60% at episode 8). Most of the ensemble's extra questions come in episodes 6–8, after it has already been moving the wrong way; its alarms come from the risk model retrained on the new data, not from noticing the change. ConformalDAgger's questions come earlier and stop sooner.
  • The smaller starting margin did not help. With q0 = 0.002 the margin is too narrow (17% misses even when nothing changes) and relearning is slower. So the per-episode reset to 0.01 does not explain the gap to the paper's numbers. The remaining differences are likely in details the paper does not give (goal positions, demo noise, network size, how the ensemble's risk model is trained).

Bottom line for the reproduction: the core claims hold: calibrated margins (ConformalDAgger near its target, the ensemble missing 80–100%), and faster recovery after an expert shift. The specific "asks twice as often as EnsembleDAgger at the shift" result is not reproduced in my setup.
