AI planning · language models

Teaching a language model to plan, one checked step at a time

Language models write plans that sound right but break the rules. I rebuilt a recent MIT method, PDDL-INSTRUCT, in which an exact plan checker grades every step the model writes and then teaches it to fix its own mistakes. A small open model went from solving none of 200 test problems to 71, trained on one laptop GPU.

Full fine-tuning vs LoRA, in plain words

Full fine-tuning (paper)

Every one of the model's weights is adjusted. The optimiser must also store extra numbers for each weight, so training an 8-billion-parameter model this way typically needs around 100 GB of GPU memory or more, spread over several GPUs. The result is a complete new copy of the model.

LoRA (this rebuild)

The original weights stay frozen. Beside each layer, two small matrices are added and only they are trained (here 1.2% of the model). It fits in 8 GB, trains quickly, and the result is a small file loaded on top of the original model. It learns a new format very well; it can fall behind full fine-tuning when a lot of new knowledge is needed.

Full fine-tuning:   W_new = W + ΔW          ΔW is as big as W (all weights change)
LoRA:               W_new = W + B · A       W frozen; A and B are thin (rank 16)

1

The problem

A plan is only useful if every step is allowed at the moment it happens.

Ask a language model to stack some blocks and it will happily write stack a on c while its hand is still empty. Each step reads well; together they are impossible. This is a known weakness: on PlanBench, the standard test of planning in language models, even large models fail on many problems, and they do much worse when the words are replaced by meaningless ones, which shows they rely on familiar wording rather than logic.

Planning has a precise language, PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language). A PDDL file lists the facts that can be true, and for each action the facts it needs (preconditions) and the facts it changes (effects). Given a PDDL problem, a computer can check any plan exactly. The PDDL Basics page explains it with pictures, and the PlanBench page describes the benchmark itself.

2

The idea

Let a perfect checker be the teacher.

A plan checker (like the standard tool VAL) never makes mistakes: for each action it says whether every precondition holds, what the action changes, and whether the goal is reached. PDDL-INSTRUCT uses that checker in two ways:

As a writer of examples

It writes thousands of perfect step-by-step explanations: "this precondition holds, these facts are added, this is the new state". The model learns to reason in exactly that form.

As a grader

It grades the model's own plans and every state the model claims, and says precisely what went wrong. The model is trained to use that feedback to repair its plan.

Why it works: the model doesn't just copy answers. It learns the habit of checking each step, and because its reasoning is written out, the checker can catch the exact step where it goes wrong.

3

The method in two phases

First learn the language of checking; then learn to plan and repair with feedback.

Phase 1: instruction tuning

The model reads 1,440 worked examples: explain why a correct plan works, find the error in a broken plan, and write a plan step by step. It learns the format of logical checking.

Phase 2: learning from the checker

The model writes its own plans. The checker grades every step. Correct answers are kept; wrong ones come back with feedback and a repaired plan, and the model trains on both.

5

Why it matters for robots

A robot that explains its plan step by step can be checked before it acts.

A robot asked to tidy an office needs a plan: which door to open, what to pick up first, where to put it. If a language model writes that plan, a mistake means a robot trying to grab something it can't reach. With this method the plan comes with its reasoning, a checker can verify each step before the robot moves, and when the check fails the model gets precise feedback and repairs the plan. The same idea links to my other work on semantic safety and human-robot interaction.

Source paper: P. Verma et al., with Julie Shah (MIT), Teaching LLMs to Plan: Logical Chain-of-Thought Instruction Tuning for Symbolic Planning, arXiv 2509.13351, 2025. The authors did not release code; everything here, from the checker to the training, was written from scratch.
